How to use this kit

You don't have to do all of this at once. The roadmap on the next page spreads it across four weeks. Print what you need, when you need it.

Three habits that make the rest easier

1. Keep one binder. Everything from this kit goes in one place, in this order. When another family member steps in, you hand them the binder, not a phone call.

2. Write down every call. Insurance, pharmacy, the doctor's office. Date, who you spoke to, what they said. The call log at the back exists because you will be asked "who told you that?"

3. Bring the medication list everywhere. Every provider will ask. A current list in your hand is the single most useful thing you can carry into an appointment or an emergency room.

The plan

30-day roadmap

Four weeks, one focus each. Check things off as you go. If a week slips, it slips. The order matters more than the pace.

Week 1 · Stabilize

Make sure anyone who walks in the door can keep your parent safe.

  • Fill in the emergency fridge sheet and tape it up
  • Build the current medication list (bottles in hand, not from memory)
  • Fill in care team & contacts
  • Confirm the pharmacy and set up refill reminders
  • Start the weekly medication log
  • Save the primary doctor's after-hours number in your phone

Week 2 · Paperwork

Find out what exists before you need it in a hurry.

  • Work through the important documents checklist
  • Locate insurance cards, Medicare card, photo ID; photograph both sides
  • Ask whether a healthcare power of attorney and advance directive exist
  • Ask the doctor's office which release form lets them talk to you
  • List recurring bills and how each one is paid

Week 3 · Care team & routine

Turn a crisis into a schedule.

  • Book or confirm the next appointment with every current provider
  • Fill in "Questions to ask the doctor" before the first visit
  • Write down the daily routine: meals, meds, sleep, activity
  • Decide who does what among family; write names next to tasks
  • Ask the doctor's office what to watch for and when to call
  • Start the daily care log if anything is being monitored

Week 4 · Home, money, and you

The things that go wrong quietly.

  • Do the home safety walkthrough, room by room
  • Fix the three cheapest, highest-risk items first
  • Set up the call & decision log as your running record
  • Ask what local help exists: Area Agency on Aging, senior center, respite care
  • Do your first weekly caregiver check-in
  • Put one thing on the calendar that is only for you

At the end of 30 days you should have

A fridge sheet anyone can act on · a medication list you trust · the location of every important document · a care team with a next appointment · a home you've walked through with fresh eyes · and a weekly five minutes for yourself.

Week 3 · fill in the night before

Questions to ask the doctor

Check the ones that apply, add your own, and write the answers during the visit. Hand the sheet to the doctor if it's easier than reading it aloud.

Appointment with: ____________________   Date: ____________

About the condition

  • What is the diagnosis, in plain words?
  • What should we expect over the next few months?
  • What changes should we watch for, and which ones mean we call you right away?
  • Is there anything we should stop doing, or start doing, at home?

About medications

  • Here is the full list. Does anything on it concern you?
  • What is each new medication for, and how will we know it's working?
  • What side effects are common, and which ones should we report?
  • Is there anything that should not be taken together, or with food or alcohol?

About care and support

  • Who do we call after hours, and when should we go to the ER instead?
  • Would home health, physical therapy, or other services help? Who orders them?
  • Is it still safe for them to drive / live alone / manage their own medications?
  • Which release form lets you talk with me about their care?
  • When is the next visit, and what should we bring?

Week 4

Home safety walkthrough

Walk through the house the way your parent moves through it: from the bed to the bathroom at night, from the chair to the kitchen. Check what's fine, note what isn't.

Everywhere

  • Loose rugs removed or taped down
  • Clear paths, cords off the floor
  • Night lights bedroom → bathroom
  • Smoke & CO detectors tested
  • Emergency numbers posted (fridge sheet)
  • Phone reachable from bed and chair
  • Thermostat easy to read and reach

Entry & stairs

  • Sturdy handrails on both sides
  • Steps even, edges visible, lit
  • Door locks they can work easily
  • A neighbor or lockbox has a key

Bathroom

  • Grab bars by toilet and in shower
  • Non-slip mat in tub or shower
  • Shower chair or bench if standing is hard
  • Raised toilet seat if needed
  • Water heater set to 120°F or lower
  • Door can be unlocked from outside

Bedroom

  • Lamp reachable from bed
  • Bed height lets feet touch the floor
  • Clear path to the bathroom
  • Glasses, phone, water within reach

Kitchen

  • Everyday items at waist to shoulder height
  • No step stools needed for daily use
  • Stove knobs clearly marked; auto-off if forgetful
  • Fire extinguisher within reach
  • Expired food and medicine cleared out

Medications

  • One storage place, not scattered
  • Weekly pill organizer if more than 3 meds
  • Old prescriptions removed
  • List on the fridge matches the bottles

Warning signs worth taking seriously

  • Snapping at my parent or family
  • Skipping meals or living on coffee
  • Not sleeping even when I can
  • Dreading the phone ringing
  • Nobody else knows the routine
  • Haven't seen a friend in weeks

Three or more checked for two weeks running: that is the moment to call someone. Your doctor, a caregiver support line, your Area Agency on Aging, or the person on the contacts page you trust most.
